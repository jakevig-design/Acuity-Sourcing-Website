Industrial · Spin-off
Newly Independent Global Technology Company
Spin-off Separation · $750M Engineering Applications Portfolio

A Fortune 500 industrial company announced a three-way corporate split. Of the three new entities, the energy business was by far the most complicated to stand up: sixteen separate business units, each with its own systems, processes, and culture, unified for the first time into a single independent company with a 130-year heritage but no independent sourcing function. The new entity inherited a $750M engineering applications portfolio, including core tools for product lifecycle management, computer-aided design, and simulation. Every master agreement in that portfolio needed to be separated, transferred, or renegotiated before the split closed, without interrupting the operations of either the parent or the new company.

The work required building the sourcing function in parallel with running it. I led contract separation across 40+ master agreements, negotiating directly with vendors to preserve commercial terms, minimize transfer and assignment fees, and secure operational continuity through the transition. In parallel, I helped stand up the new company's sourcing processes, dashboards, and vendor management framework from scratch, and worked across the sixteen business units to rationalize license entitlements and suppliers that had accumulated over decades of independent operation.

The separation closed on schedule. Separation and transfer costs were mitigated by $34M across the portfolio. The new sourcing function was operational from day one of independence, with the dashboards and data that supported the transition continuing to drive strategic conversations with stakeholders well after the split.

Financial · Asset Management
Global Asset Management Firm
Trading Platforms and M&A Integration · $85M Annual Software Spend

A top-tier asset manager running a growing portfolio of trading platforms faced two simultaneous pressures: normalizing vendor agreements acquired through multiple M&A transactions, and controlling cost escalation across a spend category that had grown faster than any other line item in the IT budget. The existing contract population was fragmented, commercial terms varied wildly depending on when and how each vendor had been acquired, and the firm's standard governance model applied strict information discipline to every deal, with negotiations timed around earnings windows, press blackouts, and investor communications.

I managed the $85M annual spend across trading platforms and M&A-acquired software. The strategy was two-pronged. Commercial negotiation focused on reducing unit cost across renewals and normalizing commercial terms across acquired agreements. In parallel, demand management targeted the consumption side of the equation, identifying entitlements that had been committed but under-utilized and either redeploying them internally or restructuring them at renewal. The work required close coordination with front-office stakeholders who had strong opinions about the tools they used and limited patience for procurement process.

The work delivered $20M in negotiated savings across the portfolio and an additional $10M through demand management and entitlement discipline. Beyond the numbers, the work established a template for how trading platform vendors would be managed on an ongoing basis, including quarterly business reviews and a vendor scorecard framework that gave the firm a continuous read on performance and commercial leverage.

Financial · Analytics
Global Financial Analytics Firm
Post-Audit ITAM Program Buildout · $500M+ in Software Assets

Following a significant software audit that exposed compliance gaps and resulted in material fines, the firm made software asset management a top-down priority. The audit had surfaced the limits of a decentralized, spreadsheet-driven approach to entitlements, and leadership wanted a proper Software Asset Management function built from the ground up, with defensible reporting and an ITAM platform that could scale with the firm's aggressive M&A activity.

I led the RFP to select the ITAM platform, ran the implementation, and served as the subject matter expert for Microsoft licensing through the buildout, the most complex entitlement position in the portfolio and the one with the highest audit exposure. While the program was being stood up, the firm was audited a second time, this time by Adobe. The newly implemented ITAM function was used live, under pressure, to defend the position and negotiate the settlement. The outcome of that audit was a negotiated resolution and, in the course of it, one of Adobe's first SaaS contracts, negotiated on behalf of the firm before SaaS had become the default commercial model for most enterprise software.

The completed program managed over $500M in software assets and mitigated $60M of audit exposure across the firm's enterprise software portfolio. The Microsoft Enterprise Agreement, renegotiated during the same period, delivered a 15% reduction in annual spend while accommodating rapid headcount and acquisition growth.

Financial · Analytics
Global Financial Analytics Firm
Microsoft Enterprise Agreement Negotiation During M&A Growth

During a period of aggressive acquisition and expansion, the firm's Microsoft exposure was growing faster than its ability to forecast it. SQL licensing rules had just changed in a way that materially increased cost per seat, and Google was actively pitching leadership on Google Docs as an enterprise alternative. The renewal was on the table, the price increase was significant, and the path of least resistance was to accept the vendor's terms and move on.

The approach was to build the story before negotiating the number. We quantified the unexpected cost increase driven by acquisition activity, documented what had already been done internally to reduce exposure, laid out a benchmarked view of what the deal should look like, and produced an honest analysis of why Google Docs was not a viable enterprise alternative in the firm's environment. That work, presented to leadership and then to Microsoft, changed the shape of the conversation. We traded in unused licenses to right-size the entitlement, sequenced the purchase of specific license types to maximize available incentives, and negotiated around the new SQL rules rather than absorbing them at list.

The renewal contained the annual cost increase to $1.3M. Material, but far below the vendor's initial ask, and below what the M&A-driven growth would have produced without intervention. Leadership approved the outcome because the story supporting the number was complete, benchmarked, and honest about what was possible.

Publishing · IT Operations
Global Publisher
Procure-to-Pay System Buildout · $1M First-Year Savings

A mid-sized global publisher was running its IT procurement through a fragmented set of processes: purchase orders approved by email, invoices tracked in spreadsheets, GL codes applied after the fact, and a chronic problem of IT budget overruns that finance could not explain. The CIO wanted the function fixed. I reported directly to the CIO and was responsible for all things IT and money, working closely with the finance department on budget and intercompany allocations.

The work was systems thinking, not negotiation. I designed and implemented a Procure-to-Pay system built around three linked controls: a simple policy requiring manager approval and a valid GL code for every request, a three-way match linking helpdesk tickets to POs to invoices, and a digital document workflow so invoices were scanned and filed before physical submission to AP. CIO and CFO sponsorship made adoption straightforward. The system was live within weeks.

The system delivered $1M in first-year savings through a combination of eliminated maverick spend, correctly allocated charges, and the elimination of duplicate invoicing. More importantly, it gave the CIO a real-time view of IT spend against budget for the first time, and it became the foundation for the firm's ongoing IT asset refresh program.

Financial · Asset Management
Global Asset Management Firm
Proportional Vendor Onboarding for a High-Velocity Category

Enterprise vendor onboarding at the firm had been designed around the highest-risk, highest-complexity software purchases, and it applied the same process uniformly across the entire category. For trading platforms, where integrations were often small-scale, time-sensitive, and posed a different risk profile than enterprise-wide deployments, the standard process was a meaningful drag. Traders waited weeks for tools that generated none of the risks the process was designed to catch, and the sourcing team administered friction that added no commercial value.

The work was a process redesign built on a careful audit of what each step of the standard workflow was actually protecting against. I identified the reviews that were load-bearing for the category (commercial terms, information security for trading integrations, regulatory applicability) and the reviews that were not (the full enterprise-wide controls appropriate to a multi-year Tier 1 SaaS deployment). I then built a modified onboarding path that preserved the load-bearing reviews, cut the rest, and routed work to the right internal reviewers on a faster timeline. The redesign required rebuilding the Coupa workflow and taxonomy to support the split, and socializing the change with legal, InfoSec, and the sourcing team so the category-specific path was understood and trusted.

The redesigned workflow reduced vendor onboarding time for the category by more than 60%. Beyond the time savings, the work introduced a proportional-process principle that was later applied to other categories where the risk profile did not justify the full enterprise-wide workflow.

Central Bank · Research
Central Bank Research Institution
Sourcing Policy Training Program

The institution operated under a 50+ page acquisition policy written by policy makers, for policy makers. Exhaustive, legally defensible, and genuinely hard to read for the employees who were nonetheless accountable to it. Most policy violations were not willful. They were the result of good-faith employees unable to decode what the policy actually required of them in everyday situations, and handling decisions (a t-shirt order, a small vendor quote, a friend-of-a-friend referral) in ways that crossed lines they did not know existed.

I designed and delivered a two-hour sourcing policy training, running it twice a year with roughly 30 participants per session. Rather than walking through the policy clause by clause, I opened with case studies I built from scratch: everyday scenarios where ostensibly benign activities were actually policy violations. Participants worked through the cases in small groups, surfaced the issues they saw, and then I layered in the specific policy requirements that applied. The close was a Q&A session and a return to work with a better intuition for the spirit behind the rules.

The program was well received by both leadership and participants, and ran continuously through my tenure. The framing, we are all accountable to the policy, and sourcing is not the police, changed how employees approached compliance questions and reduced the volume of informal escalations to the sourcing team.
